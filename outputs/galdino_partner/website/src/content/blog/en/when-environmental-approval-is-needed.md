Environmental questions should be raised while planning a business, before machinery arrives or premises are renovated. An apparently simple activity may have different requirements when its capacity, materials or location changes.

## Start with the operating facts

Record products, capacity, processes, raw materials, water, energy, waste sources and location. This is more useful than immediately requesting an “environmental permit package”. Basic data also help specialists explain which issues need further assessment.

Separate current conditions from planned expansion. Older documents should be checked against the proposed activity rather than simply retained in an archive.

## Understand the document pathway

The environmental management framework includes instruments requiring different levels of assessment. SPPL, UKL-UPL and AMDAL are not selected solely by cost or speed. Activity type, scale, location and potential impacts form part of the screening.

General explanations cannot determine an individual project’s route. Screening requires project data and the relevant sector requirements.

## Connect requirements to facility design

Wastewater control, emissions, material storage and waste management may affect layout and technical work. Operations, engineering and licensing teams should therefore share assumptions before design decisions are fixed.

For example, increased production capacity may increase utility needs and change environmental data. Record the change during planning so assessments do not lag behind the design.

## Helpful initial records

- Activity description and process diagram.
- Capacity and main equipment list.
- Location and site plans.
- Water, energy and discharge information.
- Previous environmental documents.
- Proposed development schedule.

Missing information should have an assigned owner, rather than being filled with untraceable assumptions.

## Obligations after approval

Environmental work does not end when a document is issued. Create a list of management, monitoring, reporting and change-review commitments. An internal schedule helps connect administrative requirements with work actually performed at the facility.
